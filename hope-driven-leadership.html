Leading with hope

what is that all about?

What does hope mean to me?

I come from a Jewish Orthodox family, and growing up this idea of hope played a big part in my life, the belief that things can be better. There is the Chassidic nigun, a tune, that touches on a very deep level, connecting Jews across generations, and for me sounds of hope.

That is the artistic and emotive expression for me for hope.

In later years I have learned to apply planful problem solving, professionally as a product manager, as well as in managing personal areas of my life. This is where the rational part of hope comes in for me, setting goals and ways to achieve those goals, and developing skills to give me the confidence and ability to achieve those goals.

How do I apply it in the context of an organizational culture?

When working with a team, I can lead with hope. This means when I lead rituals to align on goals, or OKRs, and perform iteration, or roadmap planning to figure out how we go about those goals, I also think of people’s ability to go about these goals.

While there was a time in which I focused on technical capabilities, with time I’ve learned to also consider psychological safety, or a sense of belonging, that is as well necessary for people to feel empowered to go about the plan.

Resilience is also key for the team’s ability to continue going on about a goal.

The social scientist Brene Brown recognizes these three factors as required for building resilience:

  1. Cultivating hope - we have a goal and we know the path to get to it and work on our confidence to go about those paths.
  2. Practicing critical awareness - we are able to identify and express when things don’t sit right with us, disrupting the echo chamber, or just our own censor.
  3. Feeling our feelings - we make space for a wide range of feelings, also the negative ones.

Every team needs to find the right setting for the practice of building a resilient team.

Some teams might need to start with anonymized surveys to highlight what’s not working and through surveying psychological safety can work to reach the team becoming a space space.

In other cases team members can have 1:1 with other members to build that sense of healthy divergence and convergence in a team, when people have difficulty seeing and valuing each other.

When the team feels like a safe place, a setting such as sprint retrospectives, or quarterly reviews can be a good chance for practicing critical awareness and acknowledging feelings as part of becoming more resilient as a team.

What do we do when things don’t go according to plan?

I can try to anticipate what might go wrong and prepare for different scenarios, but the way things work we can’t plan for everything that would go wrong, and we will need to learn how to improvise at times.

If our plan fails we might need to find a new path to go about our goal, here the sense of empowerment comes into play too. This ability to find solutions and new paths to reach our goal also feeds off a nurturing environment that encourages creative problem solving, where people are safe to be themselves and bring new ideas to the table.
