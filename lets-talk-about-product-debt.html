Let's Talk About Product Debt

Over the years, I've learned how crucial it is to have honest discussions about the debt we’re carrying to understand its cost and prioritize it against feature work.

Summary: what I’ve learned

Balance

Finding the balance between solving things for our customers, improving their experience, and solving things for us, so we can continue to develop the product. In reality, we’re balancing value, risk, and effort to understand priorities and make tough calls about what we do now and what we do later.

Collaboration

As time goes by, debt can become more complex and heavier. This often means that only through collaboration between teams can we solve it. This asks us to listen and think with openness as well as criticality, while respecting the diverse perspectives and needs brought to the table, reaching convergence and moving forward with a plan.

Why Product Debt

I believe that changing our narrative from seeing debt as a technical problem to seeing it as part of our product can improve business value, make delivery more predictable, and reduce friction for our customers.
For example, when creating a new feature or product, we can define from the get-go what success and failure look like and how much we are willing to invest in validating the business idea. This helps us avoid carrying the burden of workarounds, unmaintained systems, and outdated technology.

What to do now, and what later

When we ran into compatibility issues with our installer technology, this was my biggest challenge up to that point. It taught me to find a balance, engage in collaboration, and create a product definition that addresses the debt.

I didn’t have a single tool to do that then, but I did have healthy discussions with my teams about the product debt we carry and when or whether we want to address it.

I’ve learned that healthy discussions require a safe environment where people can engage without a hierarchy of opinion. We balance different perspectives on risk, value, and effort, and agree on where more supporting data is needed to decide on the next step and the long-term plan.

Recent research

I was recently intrigued by a hackathon focused on learning how to use AI to build solutions. I took it as an opportunity to revisit this topic and talk with teams about how they experience technical debt. A common theme among my interviewees was the importance of open communication, transparency, and discussing the debt. My motivation was to find a way to prioritize this work against feature work while setting KPIs to measure our impact.

Highlights from my interviews:

  • Tech debt exists from the moment software is written, so it is part of the product definition. As part of the product lifecycle, teams discuss what to keep, improve, replace, or drop.
  • Prioritization depends on understanding the size of the need and the value of addressing it.
  • We need to define when to declare a venture a failure.
  • Tech tasks are often not assigned story points, leaving the benefits of addressing them based on intuition and difficult to quantify.
  • Good collaboration includes reserving time to address product debt and trusting teams to judge what will deliver value by reducing risk and improving delivery.
  • A side project can jump-start planned improvement work once a prototype demonstrates its value.
  • Without the agency to address product debt, teams can lose motivation, employee retention can suffer, issues can reach users, and teams can lose alignment on what they work on.
  • Taking time to engage in continuous learning and connecting work to business outcomes creates healthy discussions leading to positive business impact.
  • Communicating and making agreements around product debt lead to healthy trade-offs. For example, we can decide that the cost does not justify following best practices for a scheduled certificate renewal, and when it is high enough to prioritize the complex project of bringing a system’s maintenance in-house to gain more control over its maintainability and reliability.

Usage of AI

  • An AI agent can help us scope complexity and effort, overcoming anchoring and the social pressure to conform. Human judgment is still needed, and teams do not yet trust an agent’s judgment. Using multiple agents may help mitigate this concern.
  • While AI is useful for simple bugs, reviews, and tests, teams do not trust it to work independently.
